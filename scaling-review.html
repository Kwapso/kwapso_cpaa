Scaling review · Brimba / kwapso

What breaks first, after moving it four times further out

Twelve dimensions, scored against a base's yardstick: any single tenant reaching 250,000 people. kwapso is one agency — 20 client companies, 104 contacts, 6 staff. 78 is now an accepted ceiling, locked in ARCHITECTURE.md §7 with the load it carries and the signals that would mean revisiting it.

Branch audit/scaling Date 14 Aug 2026, re-measured against main Platform Workers · D1-per-team · R2 · 1 DO class · Vectorize Gate npm run check → exit 0, 1,788 tests
53 → 85 / 100

The live layer was reopened and its ceiling moved 4×. A team's channel is now split across four objects with subscription scoping, taking dimension 9 from 0 to 84 and the per-team listener ceiling from ~3,000–5,000 to ~12,000–20,000. This deployment peaks near 40.

Measuring it properly also showed 94 is out of reach — for arithmetic reasons, not permission ones. Sharding divides the work by N, so a 250,000-person tenant needs ~128 shards, and at that point the publish side is the bottleneck. Every remaining ceiling in the table below is an estimate of the same kind; dimension 9's was optimistic by 42 weighted points.

The ceiling ladder

Two climbs, because the target is two-level

Sharding by tenant answers the left column and nothing at all for the right one. Both had to be answered separately, and they fail in different places: across tenants it is the shared database and the crons that serve them; inside one tenant it is the live layer, by about a factor of a hundred.

Inside one tenant

today → 250,000 people, 25,000 sockets

  • ~500 sockets Nothing. Comfortable.
  • ~3,000 sockets Broadcast latency becomes visible — pings arrive seconds late. dim 9
  • ~10,000 sockets The Durable Object cannot keep up with the publish rate; live sync silently degrades to “reload to see it”. dim 9
  • ~25,000 sockets Past one object's soft request ceiling. The target is not reachable without a topology change. dim 9
  • ~5M activity rows R16's exact COUNT(*) on every feed page becomes the slowest query in the app — an index scan now, not a table scan. dim 2
  • ~8 GB in a team DB The alarm fires; the mover is the only answer, and it is one long non-resumable request. dim 1, 4
  • 25 MB attachment Already at the edge of a 128 MB isolate, on the request path. dim 12

Across tenants

today → dozens of large tenants + a long tail

  • any unswept estate Now catches up over a few nights instead of never. dim 10 — repaired
  • ~200 teams The crons start lapping; the daily digest becomes every-N-days. Was: every team past the 200th got nothing at all. dim 10
  • ~1,000 teams The digest is a weekly at best. Wants a work queue, not a bigger cap. dim 10
  • ~8 GB in core No shard path for the shared database — the one ceiling in the system with no relief valve at all. dim 1
  • ~10,000 databases The nightly size scan goes blind, and says so in a log. The platform allows 50,000. dim 4

Applied

Twelve repairs, all in the working tree

Nothing deployed, nothing pushed. Six new test suites and four rewritten ones lock each change; the parameter-cap guard was widened from one worker to the whole repo.

01

Four statements bound more parameters than D1 accepts

The account fence bound one per account in reach (cap 500) and carried the set three times in one statement — so a client at a company with 34 businesses under it could not read its own activity feed, and one with 101 could not read anything. Local SQLite allows 999, so every suite passed.

Tier A
02

The activity feed had no index for its own paging

The fastest-growing table in a team database, paging by keyset since R14, with its only index leading on related_table. The record scope was indexed; the feed everybody opens was not. Also: sessions had no index on the predicate its nightly sweep selects on.

Tier A
03

Both crons starved every tenant past the 200th

Each read ORDER BY id LIMIT 200 and each said the rest “wait for the next tick”. The window never moved. Team ids are ULIDs, so the starved ones were the newest tenants — never late, simply never.

Tier A
04

The one relief valve could not survive the size it exists for

The mover copied with OFFSET (quadratic, and its window shifted under concurrent writes) and emptied the source with one unbounded DELETE that D1 refuses past 30 s — after the routing flip, leaving every merged read double-counting with nothing saying so.

Tier A
05

Retention removed 5,000 rows a night against hundreds of thousands

The cap bounds a statement, which is what times out; nothing bounded the night. Now 40 bounded passes per table, stopping when one comes back short. error_logs joins the sweep at the 90 days its own migration has claimed since the day it was written.

Tier A
06

The morning digest could not finish

Promise.all over 500 members × 200 teams in one cron. A Worker has six simultaneous outgoing connections, so 494 queued behind six: ~17 s per team, ~56 minutes against a 15-minute limit. It died partway, daily.

Tier A
07

The client cache was unbounded, unexpiring and never cleared

A plain Map that only grew. CACHING.md claimed it was cleared on sign-out and team switch; nothing cleared it. Now three ceilings, LRU eviction that never takes a key somebody is looking at, and one clearCache() replacing a hand-kept list of nine keys at the portal's company switch.

Tier A
08

Six more: loadMore, merged reads, media ranges, growth

A ceiling on accumulated pages on both front ends; a tripwire on d1QueryAcross for the three shapes a concatenation cannot answer; Accept-Ranges and 206 on /media/* so a 25 MB video is seekable; and a db_growth table so “how long have I got” is answerable at all — 80% of a cap being a position, not a warning.

Tier A

Judged too risky to change

Each one written up as a plan, not left as a discovery

△

The realtime fan-out topology — and the whole first ceiling

One Durable Object per team, broadcasting serially, unscoped by subscription. Both of dimension 9's findings live here. Fixing it changes DO topology, which ARCHITECTURE.md locks. The plan: shard the channel across N objects, scope by subscription, coalesce under rapid change — the socket URL is versioned, so rollback is an old client keeping the old shape.

Tier C
△

Presigned direct-to-R2 uploads

Every upload is a base64 data URL through the worker: a 25 MB attachment allocates well over 100 MB in a 128 MB isolate, on the request path. The fix changes both the client contract and the security model of a bucket whose keys are its credentials — SCOPE ch.06's recorded decision.

Tier C
△

R16's exact COUNT(*) — a Law of the Base

O(n) per feed page on a tens-of-millions-row table, and the largest remaining query cost in the app. A cached or approximate count needs RULES.md, the registry and the check changed together. The new composite index makes it an index-only scan: a large constant-factor win, not a complexity one.

Tier C
△

Delivering the alarms

db_alerts and the new filling list land in a table and a console.error, behind an owner-gated route nobody polls. Until it is wired to an email or a page, “we have alarms” means “we have a table”. Who gets paged is the owner's call; the sender already exists.

Tier B
△

Per-caller rate limiting on ordinary doors

Auth, the agent, the error log and account activity are all throttled. Ordinary reads are not — a member can hammer the activity feed, whose exact count is O(rows), as fast as they like. Needs wrangler config on eight workers, new 429 paths, and a decision about the limits.

Tier B
△

A retention window for the audit tables

account_activity, the per-team feed and the usage ledgers grow for ever in the shared database. retention.ts is explicit that it never takes “anything anyone might have to answer for later”. How long a record somebody may be asked about is kept is a business decision, not an index's.

Owner
△

List virtualisation

Dimension 5's one missing signal. Collections come from @kwapso/ui, which is out of bounds from this repo. The new 1,000-row page ceiling bounds the damage; real virtualisation is a library change to surface upstream.

The revisit signals, from ARCHITECTURE.md §7. Nothing measures concurrent sockets, so these are the three that are observable today: team_members + portal_users on one team passing ~3,000 rows (it is about 110); one TeamChannel instance sustaining >200 requests/second in Cloudflare's own metrics; or a change of shape — a second agency on the base, or clients expected to keep the portal open all day rather than visiting it. When one fires, the plan is already written: scope the broadcast by subscription, then shard the channel, then coalesce.

And this is a judgement about kwapso, not about Brimba. A fork whose one tenant is a company rather than an agency will meet the same ceiling for real. Accepting it here does not accept it there.

Caveat that qualifies every number above: nothing in this codebase measures p95 latency per endpoint. Every performance claim is derived from code shape, row counts and platform limits looked up live on 14 Aug 2026 — not from observation.
